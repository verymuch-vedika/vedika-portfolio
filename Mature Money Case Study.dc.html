Overview

Problem

Branch, telephony, app and web each verified customers differently. Someone verified in one channel often had to start again in another.

Approach

Rebuilt how ID&V actually worked, SME by SME, then ran channel-specific ideation before converging on one cross-channel concept.

02

The problem

Mature Money was mid-merger with CBS (Countrywide Building Society). Identification and verification (ID&V), the process that confirms who a customer is before they can transact, had grown fragmented across the bank’s channels.

Branch, telephony, app and web each handled ID&V differently, with no unified view of a customer’s verification status. A customer verified in one channel often had to re-verify from scratch in another.

  • 1000+Customer complaints analysed
  • 4Channels, four different processes
  • 0Single source of truth

An early analysis of those complaints surfaced ID&V as a recurring frustration across every channel. The deeper problem was that no one could say how ID&V actually worked. That had to be reconstructed, within each channel and across them, largely from SME knowledge rather than documentation. Some of it ran alongside other undocumented ID&V work already in flight, which I had to find and account for rather than duplicate.

03

The challenge

  1. 01

    Bring consistency to a fragmented experience

    One ID&V process, understood and applied the same way, whichever channel a customer walks into.

  2. 02

    Protect the customers the merger puts most at risk

    Older and less digitally confident customers were most exposed to inconsistent branch identification across the two merging entities.

  3. 03

    Make telephony ID&V less costly to run

    Every unnecessary re-verification on the phone cost time, and time meant operational cost and dissatisfied customers.

Step 01

Reconstructing the as-is, channel by channel

I mapped the branch and telephony journeys myself: five telephony blueprints alone (balance enquiry, bill payment, transaction enquiry, updating an email address and related queries), plus the branch journey. Each one was built by talking to SMEs, then looped back to them to confirm it before I treated it as ground truth.

Step 02

Running the room, not just contributing to it

I curated who needed to be in each session, shaped what a 1.5 to 2 hour session would cover, and facilitated all three separately, so each channel’s friction surfaced on its own terms.

Step 03

Keeping stakeholders inside the process, weekly

Weekly decks covering progress, blockers and next steps gave the client and leadership continuous visibility, rather than a single reveal at the end.

06

From discovery to ideas

Rather than one mixed-channel session, I ran three separate ones, so channel-specific friction could surface before being asked to converge into anything shared.

From that output, plus what I knew was already in flight elsewhere and the group’s own additions, I synthesised a single unifying concept.

The unifying concept

One ID&V identity, usable across products and channels, with step-up verification only when a customer’s risk tier actually changes.

A follow-on concept development session turned this into three concepts. Rather than scoring them on impact versus effort by team consensus alone, I took the scores back to SMEs in branch, telephony and digital, to check them against how those teams experienced the friction day to day.

07

The solution

Three concepts, each scored on customer impact and business effort, and each with a short-term (3-month) and long-term (3-year) plan.

  1. 01

    Awareness of ID&V questions and documents

    Customers and colleagues often didn’t know in advance what a verification step would ask for. Surfacing it upfront reduces failed attempts and repeat contact.

  2. 02

    One customer ID&V across the bank

    One identity across products and channels. A customer verified at Tier 2 doesn’t start from zero when a Tier 3 product asks for more; they only add what the higher tier requires.

  3. 03

    Consistent language across channels and colleagues

    The same question, asked the same way on the phone, in branch or in the app, removing a subtle but real source of confusion and mistrust.

08

Reaching past scope

Any real unification concept had to account for both merging entities, not just Mature Money’s side. So I reached out to CBS’s design team. It wasn’t formally scoped, but it was necessary if the concept was going to hold up.

They couldn’t answer. Key people were on leave, and CBS’s ID&V process wasn’t documented in enough detail for their own team to explain how it would integrate after the merger.

09

Where it stands

Handed overConcepts delivered, not shipped

Designs and concepts were completed, but the workstream didn’t move into development before the engagement ended. As a service design engagement, the goal was to surface solutions and hand them to someone accountable for building them.

That handoff happened: three scored concepts and their implementation plans are in the client’s hands, with an owner identified to carry them forward.

10

Learnings

  • Discovery is load-bearing, not a preamble

    When no one has documented how a process works, finding out, SME by SME and loop by loop, isn’t the step before the real design work. It is the real design work.

  • Structure the room before you ask it a question

    Three separate channel sessions before one converged concept meant each channel’s friction got heard, instead of being flattened by the loudest voice in a mixed room.

  • A finding you can’t resolve is still a finding

    The CBS gap didn’t get designed away. Naming it clearly gave the client something real to act on, which is more useful than a concept that pretends the merger is already resolved.

Smaller things I’m taking with me

Share a visible plan early

A plan everyone can see from day one makes scope conversations far easier later.

Document my own work weekly

A weekly record of what I did made progress visible and kept my own thinking sharp.

Adapt to different working cultures

People show their work differently. Some lead with metrics, others with substance, and both need room.

Ask for help sooner

Owning a workstream independently doesn’t mean solving everything alone.

Course-correct scope openly

When the work shows the scope is wrong, saying so early beats delivering to the wrong brief.

Design the service in a transient phase

Mid-merger, the service designer’s job is the service, not the product, and holding it steady while everything shifts.
