01 · Workstream

RDR Retail Delivery Reporting

The system ZMW dealers use to report every vehicle sale, capturing retail and program type for warranty enablement, compliance and pricing.

Design

  • Swapnita Design Manager
  • Vedika UI UX and Service Designer
  • Aroma UI UX Designer

Delivery

  • Chiru Project and Dev Lead
  • Amartya Scrum Master

Client

  • Stephan Senior stakeholder
  • Butiu Jun Client stakeholder
  • Sanjeev ZMW point of contact

My role

UI UX and Service Designer, co-owning the RDR workstream and reporting to Swapnita, the Design Manager. I owned and shared design work across:

Challenges and how we solved them

  1. 01

    A daily-use system built on manual entry, inside a partially automated pipeline

    Sale data arrived automatically from each dealer’s system, but the fields that mattered most for compliance and pricing, retail type and program type, were still typed in by hand, often dozens of times a day.

    What we did

    Flagged that most manual data already existed, and proposed a gradual first step

    Chassis number, sale details and customer information already lived in dealership systems; they just weren’t connected to RDR. Dealers needed a gradual path and the legacy architecture wasn’t ready for full integration, so the business took a smaller first step: merging pre-owned (POIS) and new-vehicle inventory into RDR. A step toward fuller automation, not the automation itself.

  2. 02

    Two very different users sharing one system

    Click to expand

    What we did

    Designed for B2D and B2E as genuinely different needs

    Dealer flows were optimised for a routine, repeatable task. Employee flows carried the oversight layer (search across all dealerships, approve or reject, override) without slowing the dealer down.

  3. 03

    Complexity that multiplies fast, with no map of it

    7 retail types × up to 20 program types created a large number of paths, each with different disclaimers, required fields and downstream triggers. None of it was documented in one place. No service blueprint existed, and we had to piece the legacy system together from developer knowledge.

    What we did

    Documented both scenarios end to end before touching the redesign

    Two journey maps, a corporate fleet purchase and a personal pre-owned trade-in, mapped step by step. They became the baseline every retail-type and program-type variation was designed against.

    Made retail-type and program-type selection legible instead of a blind dropdown

    The redesign showed how each combination changes the disclaimer and which fields appear next, instead of leaving dealers to guess.

02 · Workstream

BVD Branded Vehicle Disclosure

Design

  • Swapnita Design Manager
  • Vedika UI UX and Service Designer
  • Aroma UI UX Designer

Delivery

  • Chiru Project and Dev Lead
  • Amartya Scrum Master

Client

  • Stephan Senior stakeholder
  • 3 others from the BVD team BRAMs
  • Sanjeev ZMW point of contact

Context

BVD governs what happens when a customer returns a vehicle to a dealership within a threshold window and the dealership marks it as trade-assisted: a vehicle with a disclosable history (damage, repair, or a Lemon Law brand) that has to be formally disclosed and financially reconciled before resale.

Before this project, the entire process lived in Excel, passed back and forth between dealers (B2D), the AAM (Aftersales Area Manager) and the BRAM (ZMW Regional Aftersales Manager). A dealer would search a retail, create a Trade Assist request, upload documents, and work through a calculation worksheet and settlement agreement with no dedicated interface, just a linear paper trail wrapped around spreadsheets.

The brief was to move this onto the web as its own module, with a defined three-tier approval chain.

Dealer

Creates the request

→

AAM

First-level reviewer

→

BRAM

Second-level reviewer, can edit the calculation sheet

My role

UI UX and Service Designer, co-owning the BVD workstream within the team above. I owned:

  • Working through the calculation-sheet permission logic with the product owner, starting from their first pass, then substantial back and forth to resolve exactly what each approval tier could and couldn’t edit

  • Mapping the full BRAM revision scenarios: five distinct paths depending on what BRAM edits and when

  • Designing the module’s flow from Trade Assist request creation through to final ZMWNA approval

Challenges and how we solved them

The core job was turning a spreadsheet-and-email process into a defined digital workflow, making implicit steps (who reviews what, in what order, what happens on rejection) explicit and enforceable for the first time.

  1. 01

    A financial approval chain with no single source of truth

    The calculation sheet alone carried over 20 line items (new-car funding, used-car ACV, wholesale value, tax, title and licence fees, etc), reconciled across three approval tiers by hand. On top of that sat hard compliance rules: ZMWNA sign-off before resale, open recalls fixed before payout, and one of three approved legal reasons for every buyback.

    What we did

    Made the approval chain itself visible as a status

    Every request now carried an explicit stage, so a dealer always knew where their request sat and who was holding it.

    Built the document package as a defined, trackable set

    Program Form, Calculation Sheet, Settlement Agreement, Disclosure Notice, Buyer’s Order, supporting documentation and repair orders, each with upload limits and clear ownership, replacing a loosely tracked spreadsheet-and-attachment process.

  2. 02

    Multiple people editing the same numbers, with no record of who changed what

    BRAM could revise the calculation sheet or disclosure notice even after a dealer had completed the settlement agreement, which sent it back to the AAM to review, re-sign and resubmit. The old process allowed this, but every revision meant a manual loop of emails and re-signing, with no record of what changed.

    What we did

    Distinguished “BRAM comments” from “BRAM rejects”

    At the calculation-sheet stage, BRAM could request changes and comment directly on the dealer’s worksheet, but couldn’t reject the request outright. We worked this logic through with the product owner and reflected it in the flow, so the interface only offered actions that were allowed at that stage.

    Designed for five distinct revision scenarios, not just the happy path

    A straight approval, BRAM editing and approving the calculation sheet, and BRAM revising the calculation sheet, the disclosure notice, or both after the agreement was signed, each of which sends it back for a re-sign.

    Locked submitted requests against silent edits

    Once a Trade Assist request was submitted or rejected, the dealer couldn’t modify it; they started a new request instead. That closed a gap where an approved-then-changed record could previously go untracked.

03 · Workstream

VA Vehicle Administration

Context

VA is how ZMW employees lease a company car. The process involves many parties: the employee, HR, the VA Specialist who runs it, the DMV, auction houses, title agencies and a risk committee. A linked process, OOO (Online Ordering Overseas), handles cars ordered abroad.

In 10 days, the brief was to learn the process, find where agentic AI could change it, and show the client a concrete example.

The challenge

A process with real structural imbalance. Mapping the stakeholders showed an imbalance. Employees, retirees and others leasing a car are affected the most but have the least say, and face the most friction. The VA Specialist, on the other hand, controls most of how the process runs.

Low power, high impact

  • Employee
  • Additional User
  • Retiree
  • Department User

High control · VA Specialist

  • Eligibility validation
  • Licence confirmation
  • Plate assignment
  • Exception handling
  • Damage logging
  • Auction coordination
  • Compliance flagging

Documentation existed, but none of it was usable. One document wasn’t readable at all; the other was outdated, and no designer on the project knew what it had mapped. The developers had some working knowledge, but nothing consolidated. Understanding VA meant piecing it together from scratch across systems, each interfacing with the VA Specialist manually, with no reliable map of how they connected.

  • HR · entitlement, employment status
  • DMV · registration, plates
  • Title agencies · Certificate of Origin
  • Auction houses · end-of-lease disposition
  • Governance committee · risk and suspension

How the 10 days went

  1. Days 1–7

    Making sense of what existed: reconciling documentation, confirming the current version, getting terminology straight.

  2. Days 8–9

    Building AutoMate.

  3. Day 10

    Preparing for the client.

My role

UI UX and Service Designer, co-owning the VA workstream for a focused 10-day span within the wider engagement. I owned:

Step 01

Reconciling the existing but unusable documentation into a current, trustworthy picture of the process and its terminology

Step 02

Building the power and interest matrix that placed each stakeholder group and made the imbalance visible

Step 03

Documenting persona interactions and dependencies across the full stakeholder set

Step 04

Designing AutoMate, a concept for a conversational AI assistant for the VA Specialist, built in 2 of the 10 days

The agentic-AI framing was proposed to the ZMW team by a senior Accenture colleague as a direction worth exploring. The stakeholder mapping, opportunity analysis and AutoMate concept underneath it were mine.

The proposal

The finding: not a technology gap, a structural one

Employees depend on a single role, the VA Specialist, to move anything forward.

So we designed AutoMate, a conversational AI concept for the VA Specialist, and built it as concept screens to show the client what agent-assisted VA could look like.
